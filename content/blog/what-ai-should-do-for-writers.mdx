Ask a room of writers about AI and you'll get the full weather system: excitement, dread, fatigue, and a fair amount of quiet guilt from people who use it and wish they didn't, or don't and wonder if they're falling behind.

We think most of the anxiety comes from a category error: treating "AI for writing" as one thing. It isn't. There are two products hiding under that phrase, and they point in opposite directions.

## The ghostwriter and the editor

The first product writes so you don't have to. Feed it a topic, receive prose. It's genuinely useful for text nobody was going to savor: release notes, product descriptions, the email you've written a hundred times. Its purpose is to remove the writing.

The second product exists to make _your_ writing better without becoming its author. It behaves like a great editor: it reads closely, notices what you can't see from inside the draft, and hands the pen back. Its purpose is to sharpen the writer.

The first product has a hundred vendors. Umberleaf is only interested in the second.

## What the companion does

Umberleaf's craft companion works in the margin, and reaches the page only by your hand. Concretely, that means:

- **It notices patterns.** Three sentences opening the same way. A paragraph carrying two ideas that each deserve their own. A rhythm that flattens exactly where the argument peaks.
- **It asks questions.** _Who is speaking here? What does the reader know at this point? Is this the strongest place for this scene?_ These are the questions a trusted editor asks, at whatever hour you're writing.
- **It will tighten a paragraph you've already written, if you ask.** Sharper, simpler, or shorter, shown beside your own words as a proposal, which you take or wave away. This is the one place it touches prose, and it only ever touches prose you wrote.
- **It names craft, so you keep it.** Feedback references the technique, not just the fix. The goal is that you need the companion less over time, not more. A tool that deepens dependence on itself is a tool working for the wrong side.

## What it will never do

These aren't current limitations awaiting a roadmap. They're commitments:

1. **It never originates prose.** No drafts, no continuations, no opening line supplied while you sit in front of the blank page, no "write me a paragraph about…". Revising a sentence you wrote is a service; writing the sentence you haven't is a substitution. The gap between _revision_ and _autocomplete_ is where a writer's voice lives, and voice doesn't survive automation.
2. **It never acts unasked.** Nothing is suggested until you ask for it, and nothing lands in your draft until you accept it. There is no autocomplete waiting in the next keystroke.
3. **It never scores you.** No grades, no "readability index," no gamified streaks. Craft isn't a leaderboard.
4. **Your drafts train nothing.** What you write on Umberleaf is yours, not a corpus. It goes to a language model to be read, and comes back as a note or a proposal; it is never used to train one. This is a property-rights question, and the answer isn't complicated.

## Why draw the line here

Because the drudgery in writing isn't the writing. Nobody who loves this craft is begging to outsource the sentence; the sentence is the point. What writers keep asking for is the thing that's always been scarce: a reader who pays attention before the work goes public, and tells the truth kindly.

That, it turns out, is something this technology can genuinely offer: attention on demand, at 2 a.m., without ego. But only if the product refuses, structurally, to become the author.

We've built that refusal into Umberleaf's architecture, not just its settings. The companion can propose; only you can commit. Every route it has to your page runs through your own hand accepting something, which is the difference between an editor and a ghostwriter, and it is a difference in the code, not in the marketing.
